Research design · question first

Does a declining returning-user share actually indicate declining engagement?

The central measurement challenge: a ratio can fall even when the number of returning users rises. This case tests what can—and cannot—be concluded from feature-level monthly aggregates.

Working hypothesis H1

Returning users are declining

A falling returning-user share may reflect a decrease in the absolute number of returning users.

Alternative explanation H2

New-user growth changes the denominator

Returning-user counts may increase even as their share falls when new-user counts grow faster.

Measurement boundary

Not cohort retention

Neither explanation identifies whether the same individuals return in later periods. That requires user-level longitudinal data.

These are competing descriptive explanations for the observed ratio, not preregistered causal hypotheses. The endpoint comparison supports the denominator explanation for the reported aggregates; it does not establish individual retention behavior.

Research judgment

Why the initial conclusion changed

An initial reading of the declining ratio suggested weakening retention. Decomposing the metric revealed that the reported count of returning users increased.

Measurement validity matters.

A percentage can decline while its numerator rises. The denominator—and how it changes—must be examined before drawing behavioral conclusions.

What the data cannot tell us

These are feature-level monthly aggregates, not longitudinal user histories. Returning-user share is not cohort retention and cannot establish individual return behavior.

Research process · analytical self-correction

How the initial interpretation was revised

01 · Initial reading

The percentage fell

Across the feature-month observations, returning-user share declined. An initial interpretation framed this as weaker retention.

02 · Independent challenge

Inspect numerator and denominator

An AI-assisted review prompted further checking. Comparing returning and new-user counts showed the ratio alone was insufficient.

03 · Revised conclusion

Growth changed the mix

Reported endpoints show returning users rising from 235 to 252 and new users from 30 to 81. The falling share cannot be read as an absolute decline in returning users.

04 · Open question

What about actual retention?

Individual-level return behavior remains unknown. A cohort analysis would require user identifiers and observation windows.

This is a retrospective account of the analytical workflow, not a prospective experiment or a claim that an LLM autonomously verified the findings.

Interpretation

What should product teams measure?

Report active, new, and returning users separately; track usage intensity alongside adoption; and instrument user-level cohorts before making retention claims. Treat correlations as descriptive, not causal.

How to read the results

  • Across the five features, early-versus-late returning-user share fell by approximately 7.7–9.1 percentage points.
  • Active users increased across all five features.
  • Ratings had a weak, non-significant linear relationship with returning-user share in these observations (r = −0.092; p = 0.485).
  • None of these patterns alone establish a causal effect or true user-level retention.

Methods

From raw metrics to a corrected interpretation

  1. Joined feature usage and feedback by feature and month, with catalog attributes joined by feature.
  2. Checked duplicates, missing values, and whether new + returning users equaled active users.
  3. Computed returning-user share and uses per active user for five features across twelve months (60 feature-month observations).
  4. Compared months 1–6 versus months 7–12 and evaluated the Pearson correlation with ratings.
  5. Used an independent AI-assisted review to challenge the initial interpretation; decomposed the ratio into new and returning counts.
Boundaries of the evidence

Limitations & reproducibility

  • Not cohort retention: monthly aggregate feature records do not identify individuals over time.
  • Not causal: satisfaction correlations and early/late comparisons are descriptive.
  • Dependence: repeated observations across features/months limit simple significance interpretations.
  • Selection: feedback ratings may not represent disengaged users.
  • Source data unavailable: the original course CSVs are not publicly redistributed; this HTML is an evidence presentation, not a fully reproducible analysis.
  • Course sharing: confirm permission before publishing the original figures or derived findings.
